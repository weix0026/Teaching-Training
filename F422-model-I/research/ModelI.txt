Model I
In forest planning,  Model I  is one of the foundational formulations used for  scheduling and managing timber harvests  to meet specific management objectives and achieve desired forest conditions. 
The goal is to determine an optimal harvest schedule for a forest over a fixed planning horizon.
Its unique feature is to use a large set of pre-identified prescriptions to build a LP model.

ppt/slides/slide10.xml
The basic structure of Model I
Management units: i.e., stand, stratum, … 
Many candidate silviculture prescriptions designed for each unit depending on:
Min/max rotation ages
Thinning options
Length of planning periods and planning horizons
Each prescription represents a sequence of mgmt. activities across time, which can cross multiple rotations. 
i.e., thin from bellow to BA 40 at decade 10, prescribed fire at decade 11, clearcut at decade 15, thin again at decade 19 and clearcut again at decade 25. 
Forest planning becomes an optimal assignment problem: how many acres of each forest stratum should be managed by following each prescription?

Mathematical formulations
Define all  decision variables  including the definition of  subscripts   and write out the  Model I’s mathematical formulation  using  Table 2 .  The landowner’s objective is to maximize the total net revenue from managing the entire forest during the next 60 years. This Model I formulation should include  the following components . 
the objective function
constraints for initial available areas of each forest type 
bookkeeping constraint to track the volume harvested during each decade
non-declining even-flow constraints for timber harvesting
non-negativity constraints for all decision variables

Characteristics of Model I
1. Decision variables for each land management unit each represent a management option for the unit over the entire planning horizon.
2. Decision variables for the formulation take the form:  	
X ij = # of land units of management unit  i  assigned to  j th prescription for stand  i . 
3. If rotation lengths can be short and the planning horizon is long, the number of decision variables for a single management unit can be quite large. Some systematic way of specifying the options is needed.
4. Tables like the following are often used to describe the decision variables. In this example the minimum rotation length is two periods. Each "H" in the table represents a final harvest or the end of one rotation. Thinning options are not considered in this example.

ppt/slides/slide17.xml
A Model I example
Suppose we have two forest management units  a  and  b  in a ponderosa pine forest. 
Stand  a  is currently at age class 3 (median age 25) and poorly stocked; b is fully stocked and at age class 4 (median age 35). 
Clearcut  will be scheduled at middle of a period followed by immediate regeneration. 
Forest age will be tallied at the end of a period
A 60-year planning horizon was divided into six 10-yr planning periods. 
For unit a: the youngest (oldest) age to cut during the planning horizon is 40 (60) years; do nothing option is also available.
For unit b: the youngest (oldest) age to cut during the planning horizon is 40 (50) years; do nothing option is also available.
Both stands will be managed under a 50-year rotation after the last cut within the planning horizon.
